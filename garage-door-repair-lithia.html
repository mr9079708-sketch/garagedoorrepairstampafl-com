Early warning signs

Signs You Need Garage Door Repair in Lithia

Most garage door failures give a warning first. These are the signs we hear about most often, and what each one usually means.

01

Loud bang from the garage

A loud bang usually means a torsion spring has snapped. The door will feel far too heavy to lift by hand, and forcing it can damage the opener or drop the door crooked. Secure the door, do not try to lift it, and call for a spring replacement.

02

Door reverses before it hits the floor

A door that starts down and then reverses is usually a safety sensor out of line, a sensor blocked by dirt or sunlight, or an opener force setting that is too sensitive. Check the sensors for dirt and alignment first, and call us if the door keeps reversing after that.

03

Opener runs but the door does not move

When you hear the motor run and the door stays put, the fault is usually a stripped gear, a broken drive belt or a disconnected trolley. The opener is working, but nothing is connecting it to the door, and running it again only wears the gears further.

04

Door shudders or grinds going up

Shuddering and grinding usually mean worn rollers, a bent track or dry hinges, and the friction wears the springs and opener gears while it goes on. Cleaning the track and fitting a full set of rollers is the usual fix, and it is cheaper than the opener repair that follows if it is left.

Heavy Double Doors and the Springs That Carry Them in Lithia

The double garage door is the defining feature of a Lithia home, and it is also the part that fails most often. A sixteen-foot door is heavy, it cycles often, and the springs that lift it work harder than the springs on any single door. Understanding that is the key to keeping a Lithia door working.

A double door weighs roughly twice as much as a single, and its springs carry all of that weight every time the door moves. The springs fitted by the builder are usually the lightest option that met the budget, and on a door that cycles four or five times a day they lose tension sooner than the owner expects. The first sign is a door that feels heavy when you lift it by hand, or that drifts down a few inches after you let go halfway up. The second sign is a door that opens unevenly, with one side higher than the other, which means one spring is weaker than the other. Both signs mean the springs should be replaced as a pair, and soon, because a tired spring strains the opener and the cables, and a spring that lets go can drop a heavy door without warning.

Sizing the springs correctly is the whole job. A spring that is too light will fail early and make the door heavy; a spring that is too heavy will pull the door up too fast and wear the opener out. We weigh the door on the visit and size the springs to its actual weight, not to its size, because two doors of the same size can weigh very different amounts depending on whether they are insulated, what gauge the steel is, and whether they have windows. That measurement is the difference between a spring that lasts its full life and one that fails again within a year, and it is the reason we never guess a spring size from a phone description.

The opener matters just as much on a double door. A light-duty opener fitted to a heavy double door will strain every time it runs, and the gears wear out sooner than they should. When we replace springs on a double door we check the opener as well, and we set the force limits to the door rather than leaving them at the factory default. A modern belt-drive opener with a battery backup is a genuine improvement on a heavy door: it runs quieter, it handles the weight better, and it keeps working through a power cut. If the old opener is already struggling, replacing it at the same time as the springs is usually better value than paying for two separate visits.

The rollers and the track carry the same load, and they wear in the same pattern. The original rollers on a builder-grade double door are often plastic, and they wear flat and start to grind within a few years of daily use. We fit sealed nylon rollers as standard, which run quieter and last far longer, and we check the track for bends and for bolts that have worked loose, because a track that has shifted will wear a new set of rollers out just as fast. A full set of rollers, a track clean and a balance check is a small job that adds years to a double door's life, and it is the job we recommend most often in Lithia.

Storm Season on the Alafia River: Getting the Door Ready

Lithia sits inland from the bay, but storm season still matters here, and the garage door is the largest opening in most homes on these streets. A door that is ready for a storm is a door that has been checked before the season, not during it.

The first thing to check is the wind rating. Doors fitted in the newer subdivisions are usually built to the current wind-load standards, but the doors on the older homes near the river may predate them, and a door that has been patched and repainted over the decades may be weaker than it looks. The rating label is usually on the inside of the top panel or on the side of the door, and we can read it for you and tell you whether it meets the current standard. If it does not, a replacement before storm season is the sensible time to do it, because a rated door with proper bracing is the single biggest improvement you can make to how the house handles a storm.

The second thing to check is the hardware that holds the door in its tracks. Wind pressure pushes on the whole panel, and the force goes through the rollers, the track and the brackets into the wall. Brackets that have rusted, track bolts that have worked loose, and rollers that are worn flat are all weak points, and they are the parts that fail first when the wind rises. Before storm season, check that the track is bolted tight to the wall and that the brackets are sound. We check all of this on a tune-up, and we replace any bracket or bolt that is corroded rather than leaving it to be tested by the next storm.

Wind-driven rain is the third problem, and it is the one that catches most owners by surprise. Rain driven sideways by the wind gets under the header and around the side seals, and on the streets near the river it can push water across a driveway and under the bottom seal in minutes. A door that looks sound can still let in enough water to stain the slab and rust the track from the inside. We fit heavy bottom seals and check the side weatherstripping on every visit, and where the track has already rusted we replace the affected section rather than painting over it, because rust inside a track chews through rollers and makes the door grind within months.

After a storm, the checks are simple and worth doing in order. Look at the door from outside: is it sitting level, or is one side lower than the other? A door that hangs crooked has usually lost a cable or a spring and should not be run again until it is levelled. Then run it once through a full cycle and listen. Grinding means track or roller damage; a loud bang means a spring has let go. Then look at the bottom seal and the tracks for debris and rust. If anything is wrong, secure the door so it cannot fall and call us. We secure the door first, then repair the part that failed, and we test the balance before we leave.
