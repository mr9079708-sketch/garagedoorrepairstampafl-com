Door slams down instead of closing smoothly

A door that drops fast on the way down has lost spring tension or cable support, and it is dangerous to stand under. Do not try to catch it or force it back up. Secure the area and call us, because a slamming door is a spring or cable failure that needs fixing before the door is used again.

Daylight or a gap under the closed door

A gap under a closed door, or daylight showing at the sides, usually means a worn bottom seal, a track out of alignment or a door that is no longer level. It lets in rain, dust and pests, and it usually points to a balance or track problem rather than just a seal.

Mature Oaks, Falling Limbs and What They Do to a Door

Carrollwood's trees are one of the best things about living here, and one of the hardest things on a garage door. A live oak limb is heavy, and when it comes down on a door it does more damage than most owners expect.

The damage from a falling limb is rarely just a dent. A limb that lands on a panel bends the panel and the track behind it, and a bent track makes the door run roughly and wears the rollers out. A limb that lands on the header can shift the whole track assembly, so the door no longer sits true in its opening. We see doors after storms that look as if they only need a panel, and on inspection the track is bent and the brackets have pulled away from the wall. The repair in that case is the panel, the track section and the brackets together, and we quote all of it before we start so there are no surprises once the old panel comes off.

Debris is the quieter problem. Leaves, twigs and seed pods collect in the tracks and around the bottom seal, and if they are not cleared they hold moisture against the metal. A track that stays damp rusts from the inside, and rust inside a track chews through rollers and makes the door grind within months. The fix is simple: clear the track and the seal when you clear the driveway, and wash the door down after the summer storms. It takes five minutes and it adds years to a door's life. While you are there, look at the bottom seal: if it has gone hard or cracked, it is letting water in, and a new seal is a small job that keeps the track dry.

The shade itself affects the door too. A garage that sits in shade all day stays cooler, which is good for the opener electronics, but it also stays damper, which is worse for the metal. Doors in the shadiest streets rust sooner at the bottom than doors on the sunny side of the same street, because the bottom of the door never quite dries out. Washing the door down with fresh water a few times a year and touching up any chip that reaches bare metal will slow it down a lot. If the bottom section is already badly rusted, a replacement bottom section is usually better value than a patch, because a patched bottom will rust again within a year or two.

After a storm, the checks are worth doing in order. Look at the door from outside: is it sitting level, or is one side lower than the other? A door that hangs crooked has usually lost a cable or a spring and should not be run again until it is levelled. Then look for dents and for limbs on the roof or the door. Then run the door once through a full cycle and listen. Grinding means track or roller damage; a loud bang means a spring has let go. If anything is wrong, secure the door so it cannot fall and call us. We secure the door first, then repair the part that failed, and we test the balance before we leave.

Mixed-Age Hardware: Why Measuring First Matters in Carrollwood

In a suburb where the housing spans five decades, the garage door hardware spans five decades too. That is the practical problem on a Carrollwood visit, and it is why we measure first and order second.

The brands on these streets are a catalogue of the last fifty years. Some homes have doors from manufacturers that no longer exist, and the panels, springs and rollers for those doors have to be matched by measurement rather than by name. A spring that looks right can be the wrong weight, and a roller that looks right can be the wrong diameter, and either mistake means the door runs badly or the part fails early. We measure the door, the springs and the rollers before ordering anything, and we carry the common sizes on the truck so a repair can often be finished the same day. When a part has to be ordered, we tell you on the first visit and give you a flat price for the part and the fitting before anything is ordered.

The track is often the part that gives the age away. Older doors run on track that was fitted when the house was built, and the track may have been bent, patched or re-anchored more than once since then. A track that has shifted will wear a new set of rollers out just as fast as the old set, so we check the track for bends and for bolts that have worked loose before we fit new parts. If the track needs a section replaced, we do it as part of the same job, because a new roller in a bent track is money spent twice.

The springs are the other place where age shows. Some Carrollwood garages still have extension springs, which stretch along the side of the door, while most newer doors use torsion springs mounted above the door. Extension springs are older technology and they wear differently: they stretch, they lose tension unevenly, and when they fail they can snap back with force. We replace extension springs as a pair and fit safety cables through them, so if one lets go it cannot fly across the garage. Torsion springs we size to the door's actual weight, which we measure on the visit, because a spring guessed from the door's size is the reason so many doors here fail again within a year.

The opener is the last piece of the puzzle. An old chain drive on a door with new springs will still be noisy and slow, and it may not have the safety sensors that modern openers carry. When we visit we check the opener as well as the door, and we set the force limits to the door rather than leaving them at the factory default. A modern belt-drive opener with a battery backup is a genuine improvement on these doors: it runs quieter, it handles the weight better, and it keeps working through a power cut. If the old opener is already struggling, replacing it at the same time as the springs is usually better value than paying for two separate visits.
