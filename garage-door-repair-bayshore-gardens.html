The Doors Bayshore Gardens Calls Us About Most

After years of working these streets, the pattern of faults in Bayshore Gardens is clear. It is not one dramatic failure but the slow accumulation of age: rust at the bottom, tired springs, worn rollers and openers that have simply run their course.

Rust at the bottom of the door is the most common problem we see here, and it starts small. A bubble in the paint along the bottom edge, a stain where the door meets the driveway, a bottom seal that has gone hard and cracked. Left alone, the rust eats into the panel and the bottom bracket weakens, and then the cable can pull through the bracket when the door is under load. We check the bottom of the door on every visit, and where the rust is still surface-deep we treat it and touch up the finish. Where the bottom section is rusted through, a replacement bottom section is usually better value than a patch, because a patched bottom will rust again within a year or two in this air.

Tired springs are the second most common fault. The springs on a 1960s door have often been working for decades, and even if they have been replaced once, the replacement was often a builder-grade spring that was never quite right for the door's weight. The signs are easy to spot once you know them: the door feels heavy when you lift it by hand, it drifts down after you let go, or it opens unevenly with one side higher than the other. A spring that is about to fail sometimes gives a loud bang, and after that the door will not open at all. We replace springs as a pair and size them to the door's actual weight, which we measure on the visit, because a spring guessed from the door's size is the reason so many doors here fail again within a year.

Worn rollers and bent tracks follow close behind. The original rollers on these doors were often steel, and after decades of running in a track that has collected grit and rust, they wear flat and start to grind. A door that shudders or grinds on the way up is usually a roller problem, and the fix is a full set of new rollers and a track clean, which is cheaper than the opener repair that follows if the grinding is left. We fit sealed nylon rollers as standard now: they run quieter, they do not rust, and they last far longer than the steel rollers they replace. While we are there we check the track for bends and for bolts that have worked loose, because a track that has shifted will wear a new set of rollers out just as fast.

Old openers are the fourth fault in the pattern. Many Bayshore Gardens garages still have chain-drive openers from the 1980s or 1990s, and they are noisy, slow and short on safety features. A modern opener with a battery backup and proper safety sensors is a genuine improvement, and it is often the right call when the old opener needs a repair that costs close to the price of a replacement. We will always tell you when a repair is the better value and when a replacement is, because the decision should be based on the condition of your opener, not on a sales target.

Storm Season on the Bayshore: Getting the Door Ready

Bayshore Gardens sits close enough to the bay that storm season matters here, and the garage door is the largest opening in most homes on these streets. A door that is ready for a storm is a door that has been checked before the season, not during it.

The first thing to check is the wind rating. Doors fitted in the 1950s and 1960s were not built to the wind-load standards that apply today, and a door that has been patched and repainted over the decades may be weaker than it looks. The rating label is usually on the inside of the top panel or on the side of the door, and we can read it for you and tell you whether it meets the current standard. If it does not, a replacement before storm season is the sensible time to do it, because a rated door with proper bracing is the single biggest improvement you can make to how the house handles a storm. The second thing to check is the hardware that holds the door in its tracks. Wind pressure pushes on the whole panel, and the force goes through the rollers, the track and the brackets into the wall. Brackets that have rusted, track bolts that have worked loose, and rollers that are worn flat are all weak points, and they are the parts that fail first when the wind rises. Before storm season, check that the track is bolted tight to the wall and that the brackets are sound. We check all of this on a tune-up, and we replace any bracket or bolt that is corroded rather than leaving it to be tested by the next storm.
